\title{Using Mediant Trees to Transduce Rational Approximations of Real Numbers}
\author{Jon Seymour}
\date{\today}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Given an oracle for a real number $z$ of sufficient precision, we present three contributions.
First, an algorithm that constructs a \emph{mediant tree} of increasingly precise rational
approximations to $z$: starting from a Farey-adjacent bounded pair bracketing $z$, each
iteration applies a weighted mediant step with an optimal integer weight $w$ given by a
closed-form formula, yielding a new, tighter bounded pair.
Second, an algorithm that converts the mediant tree into a \emph{parity sequence} --- a string
over $\{\OO, \EE\}$ encoding the approximation path --- and shows that for $z > 1$ this
sequence is conjectured to be almost equivalent to the Sturmian sequence with slope
$1/(1+z)$, agreeing at every position except possibly the last symbol of each finite prefix.
Third, we show how to extend both algorithms to real numbers $z \in (0, 1)$ and $z < 0$
via simple algebraic reductions to the $z > 1$ case.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The problem of approximating irrational numbers by rationals is classical.
The Stern--Brocot tree and Farey sequences provide a systematic account of
best rational approximants, and the continued-fraction expansion of a real number gives
its convergents --- the sequence of closest rational approximations with bounded denominators.

The present paper is motivated by a specific need that arises in the study of
generalised Collatz sequences: to compute, algorithmically, a sequence of increasingly precise
rational approximations to the logarithm $\log_h(g)$, where $g > h \in \NN$, together with
an explicit control over the approximation error at each step.
The theory we develop is completely general: the core algorithm handles any real number $z > 1$,
and Section~\ref{sec:extension} shows how to reduce all other real numbers to this case.

Our approach works with pairs of rational estimates that simultaneously bracket $z$ from above
and below.
We call such a pair a \emph{bounded pair} (Definition~\ref{def:bounded-pair}).
At each step we construct two new mediants from the current pair, choosing an integer weight $w$
so that the new pair is again bounded, and so that the approximation errors strictly decrease.
The central contribution is a closed-form formula for the optimal $w$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:optimal-w}), which enables efficient iteration.

We also observe that the entire construction can be encoded as a string of symbols from the
two-letter alphabet $\{\OO, \EE\}$, which we call the \emph{parity sequence}
(Section~\ref{sec:parity}).
This representation connects rational approximation theory to combinatorics on words and, in the
Collatz setting, to the odd/even parity structure of trajectories.

A striking consequence (Section~\ref{sec:sturmian}) is that the parity sequence produced by
the mediant tree appears to be identical to the Sturmian sequence with slope $1/(1+z)$
(Conjecture~\ref{prop:sturmian}, verified computationally for several irrational $z$).
The mediant tree thus provides a compressed transducer for the Sturmian sequence: it encodes
a prefix of length $N$ using only $O(\log N)$ real-arithmetic operations, after which the
symbols can be enumerated by a purely integer recursive traversal of the tree.

The algorithm requires an oracle for $z$ — a means of evaluating $\lceil oz \rceil$ to
sufficient precision at each step — but is otherwise entirely general.
Given such an oracle, the mediant tree can represent \emph{any} real number as a
parity sequence over $\{\OO, \EE\}$, making it a universal encoder from reals to binary
sequences.

\paragraph{Organisation.}
Section~\ref{sec:definitions} introduces the core definitions.
Section~\ref{sec:mediant} develops the mediant construction and the straddling inequality.

\section{Definitions and Notation}
\label{sec:definitions}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Throughout Sections~\ref{sec:definitions}--\ref{sec:sturmian}, let $z > 1$ be a fixed real
number.
Section~\ref{sec:extension} handles all other real numbers by reduction to this case.
We write $\NN$ for the positive integers and $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$, $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ for
floor and ceiling.

\begin{definition}[Estimate]
\label{def:estimate}
A \emph{rational estimate} of $z$ is any pair $(e, o) \in \NN^2$; we regard the pair as
representing the rational number $e/o$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Good estimate]
\label{def:good-estimate}
An estimate $e/o$ is a \emph{good estimate} of $z$ if
\[
  e \in \{\lfloor o \cdot z \rfloor,\, \lceil o \cdot z \rceil\},
\]
i.e.\ $e/o$ is one of the two nearest rationals to $z$ with denominator $o$.
A good estimate with $e = \lceil oz \rceil$ satisfies $e/o \geq z$ and is an \emph{upper bound};
one with $e = \lfloor oz \rfloor$ satisfies $e/o \leq z$ and is a \emph{lower bound}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
In the special case $z = \log_h(g)$ with $g, h \in \NN$, the upper-bound condition
$e = \lceil oz \rceil$ translates to the integer inequality $h^{e-1} < g^o \leq h^e$,
which is the standard condition on even-step/odd-step counts in Collatz analysis.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Bounded pair]
\label{def:bounded-pair}
Two good estimates $e_l/o_l$ and $e_u/o_u$ form a \emph{bounded pair} if
$e_l/o_l$ is a lower bound, $e_u/o_u$ is an upper bound,
\[
  \frac{e_l}{o_l} < z \leq \frac{e_u}{o_u},
\]
and they satisfy the \emph{Farey adjacency condition}
\[
  e_u \cdot o_l - e_l \cdot o_u = 1.
\]
\end{definition}

The Farey adjacency condition ensures that the pair lies on adjacent branches of the
Stern--Brocot tree and that no rational with smaller denominator lies strictly between
$e_l/o_l$ and $e_u/o_u$.

\begin{definition}[$\lambda$ and $\varepsilon$]
\label{def:lambda-eps}
For an estimate $e/o$ of $z$, define the \emph{relative ratio}
\[
  \lam = \frac{e/o}{z}
\]
and the \emph{relative error}
\[
  \eps = \lam - 1 = \frac{e/o}{z} - 1.
\]
An upper-bound good estimate satisfies $\lam \geq 1$ (so $\eps \geq 0$);
a lower-bound good estimate satisfies $\lam \leq 1$ (so $\eps \leq 0$).
In both cases $|\eps|$ is small when $o$ is large.
\end{definition}

We will index estimates by $i, i+1, j, k$ and write $\lam_i$, $\eps_i$, etc.\ for the
corresponding values.
In a bounded pair we always orient so that $|\eps_i| > |\eps_{i+1}|$, i.e.\ $r_{i+1}$
is the better estimate of the two.

\begin{definition}[$\Delta$ and $K$]
\label{def:delta-K}
Given a bounded pair $(r_i, r_{i+1}) = (e_i/o_i,\, e_{i+1}/o_{i+1})$ with
$|\eps_i| > |\eps_{i+1}|$, define
\[
  \Dval = e_{i+1} o_i - e_i o_{i+1}
\]
and
\[
  \Kval = o_{i+1}(e_{i+1} - z \cdot o_{i+1}).
\]
\end{definition}

The Farey adjacency condition in Definition~\ref{def:bounded-pair} requires
$e_u \cdot o_l - e_l \cdot o_u = 1$, which is precisely $|\Dval| = 1$
(the sign depends on which of $r_i$, $r_{i+1}$ is the upper bound).
One checks that $|K| \leq 1$ for any good estimate with $|\eps_{i+1}|$ small, and that
$\operatorname{sgn}(\Kval) = \operatorname{sgn}(\Dval)$ throughout the iteration.

\paragraph{Domain restriction.}
The definitions in this section assume $z > 1$.
Section~\ref{sec:extension} shows how to reduce all other real numbers to this case.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The Mediant Construction}
\label{sec:mediant}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The classical \emph{mediant} of two fractions $e_i/o_i$ and $e_{i+1}/o_{i+1}$ is
$(e_i + e_{i+1})/(o_i + o_{i+1})$.
We generalise this to a weighted family parametrised by a positive integer $w$.

\begin{definition}[Weighted mediants $j$ and $k$]
\label{def:mediants}
Given a bounded pair $(r_i, r_{i+1})$ and a positive integer $w \geq 1$, define
\begin{align*}
  r_j &= \frac{e_i + (w-1)\,e_{i+1}}{o_i + (w-1)\,o_{i+1}}, \\[4pt]
  r_k &= \frac{e_i + w\,e_{i+1}}{o_i + w\,o_{i+1}}.
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Mediant convergence]
\label{thm:mediant-convergence}
Let $(r_i, r_{i+1})$ be a bounded pair for $z$ with $|\eps_i| > |\eps_{i+1}|$, and let
$w \geq 1$ be a positive integer such that $(r_j, r_k)$ brackets $z$ (i.e.\
$(\lam_j - 1)(\lam_k - 1) \leq 0$).
Then
\[
  |\eps_j| \leq |\eps_i| \qquad \text{and} \qquad |\eps_k| < |\eps_{i+1}| < |\eps_i|.
\]
(When $w = 1$, $r_j = r_i$ exactly so $|\eps_j| = |\eps_i|$; otherwise $|\eps_j| < |\eps_i|$.)
Moreover $(r_j, r_k)$ is itself a bounded pair.
The two mediants are assigned as $r_{i+2}$ and $r_{i+3}$, where $r_{i+3}$ is
whichever of $r_j$, $r_k$ has the smaller $|\eps|$ (the better estimate), and
$r_{i+2}$ takes the other.
Since $r_k$ always satisfies $|\eps_k| < |\eps_{i+1}|$, we have
$|\eps_{i+3}| < |\eps_{i+1}|$ regardless of which candidate is assigned to $r_{i+3}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The denominators satisfy $o_j = o_i + (w-1)o_{i+1} < o_i + w\,o_{i+1} = o_k$, so
$r_j$ and $r_k$ are distinct.
The ratio $\lam_j$ is a weighted combination of $\lam_i$ and $\lam_{i+1}$:
\[
  \lam_j = \frac{o_i\,\lam_i + (w-1)o_{i+1}\,\lam_{i+1}}{o_i + (w-1)o_{i+1}}.
\]
When $w = 1$ this gives $\lam_j = \lam_i$ exactly, so $|\eps_j| = |\eps_i|$.
When $w \geq 2$, $\lam_j$ is a strict convex combination of $\lam_i$ and $\lam_{i+1}$,
lying strictly between them, so $|\lam_j - 1| < |\lam_i - 1|$, i.e.\ $|\eps_j| < |\eps_i|$.
For $r_k$ (weight $w \geq 1$), the combination is always strict, so
$|\lam_k - 1| < |\lam_i - 1|$ and $|\lam_k - 1| < |\lam_{i+1} - 1|$,
i.e.\ $|\eps_k| < |\eps_{i+1}| < |\eps_i|$.
The Farey adjacency condition for $(r_j, r_k)$ follows by direct expansion:
\begin{align*}
  e_k o_j - e_j o_k
    &= (e_i + w\,e_{i+1})(o_i + (w-1)o_{i+1})
     - (e_i + (w-1)e_{i+1})(o_i + w\,o_{i+1}) \\
    &= \bigl[(w-1) - w\bigr]e_i\,o_{i+1}
     + \bigl[w - (w-1)\bigr]e_{i+1}\,o_i \\
    &= -e_i\,o_{i+1} + e_{i+1}\,o_i = \Dval,
\end{align*}
so $|e_k o_j - e_j o_k| = |\Dval| = 1$, and the Farey adjacency of $(r_j, r_k)$
is inherited directly from that of $(r_i, r_{i+1})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Straddling inequality]
\label{cor:straddling}
Under the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:mediant-convergence},
\[
  \eps_j\,\eps_k < 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad |\eps_j||\eps_k| < |\eps_i|\,|\eps_{i+1}|.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality holds because $r_j$ and $r_k$ straddle $z$, so their errors
have opposite signs.
For the second: $|\eps_j| \leq |\eps_i|$ and $|\eps_k| < |\eps_{i+1}|$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:mediant-convergence}), so
$|\eps_j||\eps_k| \leq |\eps_i||\eps_k| < |\eps_i|\,|\eps_{i+1}|$.
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The Optimal Weight Formula}
\label{sec:optimal-w}
%------------------------------------------------------------

A key question is: for which integer $w$ is the resulting bounded pair $(r_j, r_k)$ the
most accurate, i.e.\ which $w$ minimises the larger of $|\eps_j|$ and $|\eps_k|$?

The real-valued optimum is found by differentiating the left-hand side of the straddling
inequality with respect to $w$ and setting the derivative to zero.
Recall that $r_j = \mathrm{Mediant}(r_i, r_{i+1}, w-1)$ and $r_k = \mathrm{Mediant}(r_i, r_{i+1}, w)$,
so $\lam_j - 1 = \eps_j$ and $\lam_k - 1 = \eps_k$ are functions of $w$.
Computing the derivative gives
\[
  \frac{d}{dw}\text{LHS} =
    \frac{\Dval}{z}
    \left[
      \frac{\eps_j}{(o_i + w\,o_{i+1})^2}
      +
      \frac{\eps_k}{(o_i + (w-1)o_{i+1})^2}
    \right].
\]
Setting this to zero and solving yields the following closed-form expression.

\begin{theorem}[Optimal weight]
\label{thm:optimal-w}
The real-valued minimiser of the combined approximation error is
\[
  w = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{o_i}{o_{i+1}}
    + \frac{\Dval \pm \sqrt{\Dval^2 - \Kval^2}}{2\Kval},
\]
where
\[
  \Dval = e_{i+1} o_i - e_i o_{i+1}, \qquad
  \Kval = o_{i+1}(e_{i+1} - z \cdot o_{i+1}).
\]
The formula yields two real candidates (one for $+$, one for $-$), and the integer value of
$w$ is found by taking the floor or ceiling of each candidate and selecting the one that
satisfies $(\lam_j - 1)(\lam_k - 1) \leq 0$ (i.e.\ that the pair brackets $z$).
A full derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:optimal-w}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
When $\Kval = 0$ the formula is undefined, which corresponds to the situation where
$e_{i+1}/o_{i+1}$ is an exact rational representation of $z$ and no further improvement
is possible.
When $|\Kval|$ is small, the discriminant $\Dval^2 - \Kval^2 \approx 1$ and the two
candidates are approximately $1/(1-\Kval)$ apart, so the optimal $w$ is of moderate size.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
The iteration proceeds as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Start with an initial bounded pair $(r_0, r_1)$ for $z$.
  \item Compute $\Dval$ and $\Kval$.
  \item If $\Kval = 0$, terminate.
  \item Evaluate the two candidates for $w$ from Theorem~\ref{thm:optimal-w};
        search over $\lfloor w_- \rfloor$ and $\lceil w_+ \rceil$ for the integer $w \geq 1$
        that satisfies the bracketing condition.
  \item Form the new bounded pair $(r_j, r_k)$ and repeat from step~2.
\end{enumerate}
Because $o_{i+1}$ grows monotonically along the iteration, the denominator of $\Kval$
grows and the iteration converges (Section~\ref{sec:convergence}).

%------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The Mediant Tree}
\label{sec:tree}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Each estimate produced by the algorithm is either a \emph{leaf} — one of the
integers $\lfloor z \rfloor$ or $\lceil z \rceil$ — or a \emph{Mediant node}
built recursively from two sub-estimates.
This recursive structure defines a binary tree for each estimate, which we call
its \emph{mediant tree}.

\begin{definition}[Mediant tree]
\label{def:mediant-tree}
The \emph{mediant tree} of an estimate $r$ is defined recursively:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $r \in \{\lfloor z \rfloor, \lceil z \rceil\}$, then $r$ is a \emph{leaf node}.
  \item Otherwise $r$ is an \emph{interior node} $\mathrm{Mediant}(r_0,\, r_1,\, w)$
        with left subtree $r_0$, right subtree $r_1$, and integer weight $w \geq 0$,
        representing the rational
        \[
          r = \frac{e_0 + w\,e_1}{o_0 + w\,o_1}.
        \]
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The two leaves are the unique consecutive integers bracketing $z$:
$\lfloor z \rfloor < z \leq \lceil z \rceil$, forming the initial bounded pair
$(r_0, r_1) = (\lfloor z \rfloor, \lceil z \rceil)$ of the algorithm.
For $z = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ the leaves are $1$ and $2$.

Each bounded pair $(r_{2n}, r_{2n+1})$ in the algorithm's sequence therefore consists
of two mediant trees that share the same leaves.
As $n$ increases the trees grow deeper, recording the full history of mediant
constructions that led to each estimate.

\begin{example}[Mediant trees for $\log_2 3$]
At step $n = 1$ the bounded pair $(3/2,\, 5/3)$ has trees:
\begin{align*}
  r_{2} = 3/2 &= \mathrm{Mediant}(\underbrace{1}_{\text{leaf}},\; \underbrace{2}_{\text{leaf}},\; w=1), \\
  r_{3} = 5/3 &= \mathrm{Mediant}(\underbrace{1}_{\text{leaf}},\; \underbrace{2}_{\text{leaf}},\; w=2).
\end{align*}
At step $n = 2$ the pair $(3/2,\, 8/5)$ has trees:
\begin{align*}
  r_{4} = 3/2 &= \mathrm{Mediant}(\mathrm{Mediant}(1,\, 2,\, 1),\; \mathrm{Mediant}(1,\, 2,\, 2),\; w=0), \\
  r_{5} = 8/5 &= \mathrm{Mediant}(\mathrm{Mediant}(1,\, 2,\, 1),\; \mathrm{Mediant}(1,\, 2,\, 2),\; w=1).
\end{align*}
Every internal node carries a weight $w \geq 0$; a weight of $0$ means the left
subtree is used directly (contributing zero copies of the right subtree).
\end{example}

\paragraph{Operational use.}
The mediant tree is the practical device for generating parity sequences of arbitrary
length and precision.
Given a target $z$ and a desired precision, one runs the algorithm to depth $n$,
obtaining a bounded pair $(r_{2n}, r_{2n+1})$ whose mediant trees encode parity sequences
of length $e_{2n+1} + o_{2n+1}$.
The parity sequence $\pi(r_{2n+1})$ (Definition~\ref{def:parity-sequence}) is then
read off the tree by the recursive rule: an interior node
$\mathrm{Mediant}(r_0, r_1, w)$ with $\Delta > 0$ yields
$\pi(r_1)^w \cdot \pi(r_0)$, and with $\Delta < 0$ yields $\pi(r_0) \cdot \pi(r_1)^w$.
Deeper trees produce longer prefixes of the Sturmian sequence with slope $1/(z+1)$
(Section~\ref{sec:sturmian}).

%------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Parity Sequences}
\label{sec:parity}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The mediant construction admits a compact combinatorial encoding.

\begin{definition}[Parity alphabet and base word]
\label{def:parity-alphabet}
Let $\{\OO, \EE\}^*$ denote the set of finite strings over the two-letter alphabet
$\{\OO, \EE\}$.
For a base estimate $e/1$ (denominator $1$) define its \emph{parity sequence}
\[
  \pi(e/1) = \underbrace{\EE \cdots \EE}_{e} \OO,
\]
a string of $e$ copies of $\EE$ followed by one $\OO$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Parity sequence of a mediant]
\label{def:parity-sequence}
Given a sequence of estimates $r_0, r_1, r_2, \ldots$ produced by the algorithm,
the parity sequences of the next pair $(r_{i+2}, r_{i+3})$ are computed from the bounded pair
$(r_i, r_{i+1})$ and weight $w_{i+3}$, where $r_{i+3}$ is the better estimate and $r_{i+2}$
the other.
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $\Dval > 0$ (so $r_{i+1}$ is the upper bound, $r_{i+1} \geq z$),
    $\pi(r_i)$ appears as a suffix:
    \begin{align*}
      \pi(r_{i+2}) &= \pi(r_{i+1})^{w_{i+3}-1} \cdot \pi(r_i), \\
      \pi(r_{i+3}) &= \pi(r_{i+1})^{w_{i+3}} \cdot \pi(r_i).
    \end{align*}
  \item If $\Dval < 0$ (so $r_{i+1}$ is the lower bound, $r_{i+1} < z$),
    $\pi(r_i)$ appears as a prefix:
    \begin{align*}
      \pi(r_{i+2}) &= \pi(r_i) \cdot \pi(r_{i+1})^{w_{i+3}-1}, \\
      \pi(r_{i+3}) &= \pi(r_i) \cdot \pi(r_{i+1})^{w_{i+3}}.
    \end{align*}
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Parity sequence encodes the approximation path]
\label{prop:parity-encodes}
Any consecutive pair $(\pi(r_{i+2}), \pi(r_{i+3}))$ in the sequence uniquely
determines the initial pair $(r_0, r_1)$ and the sequence of weights $w_2, w_3, \ldots$
used at each step.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The lengths satisfy $|\pi(r_n)| = e_n + o_n$.
Given the pair $(\pi(r_{i+2}), \pi(r_{i+3}))$, the recovery proceeds as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\pi(r_i)$ is recovered by trying both cases: if the pair shares a non-trivial
    longest common suffix then $\Delta > 0$ and $\pi(r_i)$ is that suffix; if it shares
    a non-trivial longest common prefix then $\Delta < 0$ and $\pi(r_i)$ is that prefix.
    Exactly one case yields a consistent decomposition per Definition~\ref{def:parity-sequence},
    and this simultaneously recovers $\pi(r_i)$ and the sign of $\Delta$.
  \item $|\pi(r_{i+1})| = |\pi(r_{i+3})| - |\pi(r_{i+2})|$, since the two words differ
    by exactly one copy of $\pi(r_{i+1})$.
  \item $w_{i+3} = (|\pi(r_{i+3})| - |\pi(r_i)|) \;/\; |\pi(r_{i+1})|$ exactly.
  \item $\pi(r_{i+1})$ is extracted as the remaining portion of $\pi(r_{i+3})$ after
    removing $\pi(r_i)$, then divided into $w_{i+3}$ equal copies.
\end{enumerate}
Applying this recursively mirrors the Euclidean algorithm, uniquely recovering
the initial pair $(r_0, r_1)$ and all weights.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Parity sequence for $\log_2 3$]
Starting from the initial bounded pair $(r_0, r_1) = (1/1,\, 2/1)$ with $z = \log_2 3 \approx 1.58496$,
the algorithm produces the following sequence of bounded pairs.
At each step $n$, weight $w_{2n+1}$ is used to compute $r_j$ and $r_k$.
The better of the two (smaller $|\eps|$) is assigned to $r_{2n+3}$ and the other to $r_{2n+2}$,
forming the next bounded pair; $r_{2n+1}$ is always the better estimate.

\midrule
0 & $1/1$   & $2/1$   & $-0.369$ & $+0.262$ & 2 & $3/2$   & $5/3$   \\
1 & $3/2$   & $5/3$   & $-0.054$ & $+0.052$ & 1 & $3/2$   & $8/5$   \\
2 & $3/2$   & $8/5$   & $-0.054$ & $+0.010$ & 3 & $19/12$ & $27/17$ \\

The leaf words in Sturmian order are $\pi(r_0) = \mathtt{EO}$ and $\pi(r_1) = \mathtt{EEO}$.
Reading the decomposition bottom-up:

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Level 1} ($n=0$, $w_1=2$, $\Delta>0$ so suffix, $\pi(r_3)=\pi(r_1)^2\cdot\pi(r_0)$, $\pi(r_2)=\pi(r_1)^1\cdot\pi(r_0)$):
\[
\underbrace{
  \underbrace{\mathtt{EEO}}_{\pi(r_1)}
  \underbrace{\mathtt{EEO}}_{\pi(r_1)}
  \underbrace{\mathtt{EO}}_{\pi(r_0)}
}_{\pi(r_3)=\mathtt{EEOEEOEO}}
\qquad
\underbrace{
  \underbrace{\mathtt{EEO}}_{\pi(r_1)}
  \underbrace{\mathtt{EO}}_{\pi(r_0)}
}_{\pi(r_2)=\mathtt{EEOEO}}
\]

\noindent
\textbf{Level 2} ($n=1$, $w_3=1$, $\Delta>0$ so suffix, $\pi(r_5)=\pi(r_3)^1\cdot\pi(r_2)$, $\pi(r_4)=\pi(r_2)$):

  \underbrace{\mathtt{EEOEEOEO}}_{\pi(r_3)}
  \underbrace{\mathtt{EEOEO}}_{\pi(r_2)}
}_{\pi(r_5)=\mathtt{EEOEEOEOEEOEO}}
\qquad

  \;

\end{example}

\begin{remark}
In the Collatz setting ($z = \log_2 3$, $g = 3$, $h = 2$), the symbol $\OO$ in a parity
sequence corresponds to an odd step ($x \mapsto 3x+1$) and $\EE$ to an even step
($x \mapsto x/2$).
The parity sequence of a good estimate $e/o$ thus describes an idealised Collatz trajectory
with $o$ odd steps and $e$ even steps whose ratio $e/o$ approximates $\log_2 3$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Every prefix encodes the same real number]
The parity sequences produced by the mediant tree have an unusual self-consistency
property: every prefix of the sequence is itself the parity sequence of some estimate
of the same real number $z$.
That is, truncating the sequence at any point yields a shorter parity sequence that
still encodes an approximation to $z$ — not some other value.

Moreover, longer sequences encode better approximations on average: as the sequence
grows, the estimates $r_{2n+1}$ converge to $z$, so the ratio $e/o$ of $\EE$'s to
$\OO$'s in a long prefix approaches $z$ more closely than in a short one.

This monotone improvement holds only at the level of the mediant tree: moving from
one layer to the next always yields a strictly better estimate
(Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}).
Within a single layer, however, the two candidates $r_j$ and $r_k$ need not both
improve on every previous estimate — one is assigned to $r_{i+3}$ (the better) and
the other to $r_{i+2}$ (which may represent a local regression relative to $r_{i+3}$).
The strict improvement guarantee applies across layers, not within them.
\end{remark}

\section{Convergence Analysis}
\label{sec:convergence}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{theorem}[Strict decrease of $|\eps|$]
\label{thm:convergence}
At each iteration of the algorithm, $|\eps_{i+1}|$ strictly decreases.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:mediant-convergence}, both $|\eps_j|$ and $|\eps_k|$ are strictly less
than $|\eps_{i+1}|$.
The next iterate uses the better of $r_j$ and $r_k$ as its $r_{i+1}$, so
$|\eps_{i+1}^{\text{new}}| < |\eps_{i+1}^{\text{old}}|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Relationship to continued fractions]
\label{prop:cf}
The sequence of denominators $o_{i+1}$ generated by the algorithm is a subsequence of the
denominators of the convergents of $z$ in its continued-fraction expansion.
Specifically, the algorithm recovers the convergents at each step where the optimal $w$
equals the corresponding partial quotient of $z$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
The convergence of the algorithm is at least as fast as the continued-fraction convergents,
since the optimal $w$ formula ensures that no redundant steps are taken.
In practice the algorithm achieves superlinear growth of the denominator $o_{i+1}$, since
the optimal $w$ can be large (corresponding to a large partial quotient in the
continued-fraction expansion of $z$).
\end{remark}

\section{Relationship to Sturmian Sequences}
\label{sec:sturmian}
%------------------------------------------------------------

A \emph{Sturmian sequence} over $\{\OO, \EE\}$ with slope $\alpha \in (0,1)$ is defined by
\[
  s_n = \lfloor (n+1)\alpha \rfloor - \lfloor n\alpha \rfloor, \qquad n \geq 0,
\]
encoding the sequence as $\OO$ when $s_n = 1$ and $\EE$ when $s_n = 0$.
Since $\alpha < 1$, we have $s_0 = \lfloor\alpha\rfloor = 0$, so the Sturmian sequence
always begins with $\EE$.

\begin{conjecture}[Parity sequences are Sturmian]
\label{prop:sturmian}
Let $z > 1$ be irrational and let $\alpha = 1/(z+1)$.
The infinite parity sequence produced by the mediant tree is identical to the
Sturmian sequence with slope $\alpha$.

For a finite parity sequence of length $N$ (the sequence of estimate $r_{2n+1}$ at
depth $n$), the two sequences agree at every position $0 \leq i < N-1$.
At the final position $i = N-1$ they may differ: the mediant sequence always emits
$\OO$ (the terminal symbol of its last primitive word), while the Sturmian sequence
may emit $\EE$ at that position (being mid-word at depth $n$).
\end{conjecture}

The single terminal discrepancy is an artefact of truncation, not a genuine
disagreement: the mediant sequence closes its last primitive word with $\OO$ at
position $N-1$, whereas the Sturmian sequence — which continues indefinitely — may
still be in the interior of a longer word at that depth.
As the depth increases and $N \to \infty$, both sequences converge to the same
infinite word.

This has been verified computationally for several irrational values of $z$, including
$\sqrt{2}$, $\log_2 3$, $\log_2 5$, $\phi$, and $\pi$.
For $z = \log_2 3$ the verification extends to sequences of length up to $27{,}869{,}189$
symbols: at every depth the two sequences agree everywhere except possibly the last symbol,
where the mediant emits $\OO$ and the Sturmian emits $\EE$.

\begin{remark}[The mediant sequence is the better finite approximation]
At depths where the two sequences differ, the mediant's finite parity sequence is a
closer approximation to $\alpha = 1/(z+1)$ in terms of running $\OO$-density.
The terminal $\OO$ emitted by the mediant sequence gives a density slightly
\emph{above} $\alpha$, whereas the Sturmian's terminal $\EE$ gives a density
substantially \emph{below} $\alpha$.

This reflects a structural asymmetry in the two methods.
The Sturmian algorithm at position $N-1$ is mid-word in a longer primitive word,
so it emits $\EE$ — inherently favouring the above-$z$ estimate and leaving the
running density below $\alpha$.
The mediant tree, by contrast, explicitly selects at each step the estimate with
smaller $|\varepsilon|$, which is the lower-bound estimate $r_{2n}$ (the
below-$z$ member of the pair) when that is the better of the two.
Closing the sequence with $\OO$ at that point reflects this selection: it is the
correct terminal for a word whose ratio $e/o$ slightly underestimates $z$, nudging
the running density up toward $\alpha$.

For $z = \log_2 3$, the improvement at the discrepancy depths is substantial:
at $N = 1719$ the mediant density error is $1.4 \times 10^{-8}$ against the
Sturmian's $5.8 \times 10^{-4}$ — roughly four orders of magnitude better.
\end{remark}

The $\OO$-density of $\pi(r)$ for an estimate $e/o$ is
\[
  \frac{o}{e+o} = \frac{1}{e/o + 1} \xrightarrow{e/o \to z} \frac{1}{z+1} = \alpha,
\]
consistent with the Sturmian slope.

\paragraph{Efficiency and lazy generation.}
Although the parity sequences are Sturmian, the mediant tree is a far more efficient
way to generate them.
To produce a prefix of $N$ symbols, the naive Sturmian algorithm must evaluate
$s_n = \lfloor(n+1)\alpha\rfloor - \lfloor n\alpha\rfloor$ at each of the $N$
positions, requiring $O(N)$ real-arithmetic operations.

The mediant algorithm instead builds a compressed representation of the same prefix.
Each iteration requires a constant number of real-arithmetic operations to compute
the next bounded pair and weight; after $k$ iterations the tree encodes a Sturmian
prefix of length $N = e_{2k+1} + o_{2k+1}$, which grows exponentially in $k$.
Thus only $O(\log N)$ real-arithmetic operations are needed to encode a prefix of
length $N$.

Once the tree is built, enumerating the $N$ symbols requires \emph{no real
arithmetic at all}: each node specifies an integer weight $w$ and two child words,
and reading off the sequence is a recursive depth-first traversal governed entirely
by integer counts.
No auxiliary memory beyond the call stack is required.

\paragraph{$\OO\OO$-avoidance.}
Since $z > 1$ implies $\alpha = 1/(z+1) < 1/2$, the Sturmian sequence avoids
$\OO\OO$: two consecutive $\OO$'s would require
$\lfloor(n+2)\alpha\rfloor - \lfloor n\alpha\rfloor = 2$, but $2\alpha < 1$ makes
this impossible.
For comparison, when $\alpha > 1/2$ (i.e.\ $z < 1$) consecutive $\OO$'s are
unavoidable — for example $\alpha = 2/3$ gives $\OO\OO\EE\OO\OO\EE\cdots$
immediately.
The mediant tree gives an independent structural proof: at the leaves each base word
is $\EE^k\OO$ for some $k \geq 1$, and at each interior node exactly one copy of
$\pi(r_i)$ is emitted with at least one copy of $\pi(r_{i+1})$ on each side, so
$\pi(r_i)$ is never adjacent to itself; by induction $\OO\OO$ never arises.

\begin{remark}[Sturmian sequences are more restricted than general Collatz sequences]
A Sturmian sequence with slope $\alpha < 1/2$ consists of exactly two distinct words:
$\EE^{\lfloor 1/\alpha \rfloor - 1}\OO$ and $\EE^{\lceil 1/\alpha \rceil - 1}\OO$,
concatenated in the Sturmian pattern.
For slope $\alpha = 1/(z+1)$ these are precisely $\EE^{\lfloor z \rfloor}\OO$ and
$\EE^{\lceil z \rceil}\OO$ — exactly the leaf words of the mediant tree for $z$.
A Sturmian sequence can never contain a word of any other length.

A general Collatz parity sequence is far less constrained: an actual trajectory can
produce $\EE\OO$, $\EE\EE\OO$, $\EE\EE\EE\OO$, and so on — $\mathsf{E}$-runs of
any length, interleaved freely.
The Sturmian sequence is the \emph{ideally balanced} special case, constrained
to exactly the two word lengths $\EE^{\lfloor z \rfloor}\OO$ and $\EE^{\lceil z \rceil}\OO$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The weights $w_{2n+1}$ are related to the partial quotients of the continued fraction
expansion of $z$, though they are not in general identical to them.
A large weight at step $n$ corresponds to a large partial quotient, producing a long
run of one symbol type in the parity sequence — the same phenomenon that gives rise
to the irregular spacing of $\OO$'s and $\EE$'s in an irrational Sturmian sequence.
Conversely, small weights correspond to small partial quotients, and the parity
sequence advances by only a short block at each such step.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The $\log_2 3$ case]
For the Collatz application $z = \log_2 3$, the limiting $\OO$-density is
\[
  \frac{1}{z+1} = \frac{1}{\log_2 3 + 1} = \frac{1}{\log_2 6} = \log_6 2 \approx 0.3869.
\]
The convergents of $\log_2 3$ are $2/1, 8/5, 19/12, 84/53, \ldots$,
and these appear as the $r_{2n+1}$ values in Table~\ref{tab:log23}.
In the Collatz context, the $\OO$ symbols represent odd steps ($x \mapsto 3x+1$)
and the $\EE$ symbols represent even steps ($x \mapsto x/2$).
An $\OO$-density of $\log_6 2 = 1/\log_2 6$ describes the \emph{ideally balanced}
Collatz parity pattern: the asymptotic ratio of odd to even steps in a trajectory
that descends as quickly as theory permits.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The $g = h^2 - 1$ family]
For the generalised Collatz system with odd step $x \mapsto gx + 1$ and even step
$x \mapsto x/h$, the Sturmian slope is $1/(\log_h g + 1) = 1/\log_h(gh)$.
The special family $g = h^2 - 1$ satisfies $\log_h g \in (1, 2)$ for all $h \geq 2$,
so the slope $1/\log_h(gh) \in (1/3, 1/2)$ for every member.
The first convergent of $\log_h g$ from above is always $2/1$, corresponding to
the parity sequence $\mathtt{EEO}$ (two $\EE$'s, one $\OO$) and the minimal cycle
$\{h^2, h, 1\}$ shared by every system in this family.
\end{remark}

\section{Extension to $0 < z < 1$}
\label{sec:extension}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The algorithm of Sections~\ref{sec:mediant}--\ref{sec:parity} requires $z > 1$.
We now show how to handle $z \in (0, 1)$ by algebraic reduction, in two stages.

If one were content to accept $\OO\OO$ in the parity sequence, no special treatment
would be needed: the algorithm and the Sturmian definition extend formally to any
$z > 0$, and for $z < 1$ the slope $\alpha = 1/(1+z) > 1/2$ simply makes $\OO\OO$
unavoidable.
The reductions below are necessary precisely because we require parity sequences
that are valid Collatz parity sequences — and a Collatz trajectory with two
consecutive odd steps is impossible (after an odd step $x \mapsto 3x+1$ the result
is even, so the next step must be even).
The reductions map $z \in (0,1)$ to $z' > 1$, yielding an $\OO\OO$-free sequence
whose $e$ and $o$ counts then determine a rational approximant to the original $z$.

\subsection{Case $0 < z < \tfrac{1}{2}$}

For $z \in (0, \tfrac{1}{2})$, define
\[
  z' = \frac{1-z}{z} = \frac{1}{z} - 1 > 1.
\]
Run the algorithm on $z'$.
A bounded pair $e/o \approx z'$ satisfies $e/o \approx (1-z)/z$, so
\[
  z \approx \frac{o}{e + o}.
\]
The approximant to $z$ is therefore $o/(e+o)$, with numerator $o$ (the $\OO$-count
of the parity sequence) and denominator $e + o$ (its total length).

\paragraph{Parity sequence.}
The parity sequence for $z'$ has $\OO$-density
\[
  \frac{1}{1 + z'} = \frac{1}{1 + (1-z)/z} = z,
\]
which is less than $\tfrac{1}{2}$ since $z < \tfrac{1}{2}$.
Consequently the $\OO\OO$-avoidance result of Section~\ref{sec:sturmian} applies:
the parity sequence for $z'$ is $\OO\OO$-free, and its $\OO$-density converges to $z$.

\paragraph{Sturmian identity.}
The Sturmian sequence with slope $\alpha' = z$ is the infinite parity sequence
produced by the mediant tree for $z'$ (Conjecture~\ref{prop:sturmian}).
This Sturmian sequence encodes $z$ directly: the $\OO$-density of any long prefix
approximates $z$ with the same precision as the underlying mediant approximation
of $z'$.

\subsection{Case $\tfrac{1}{2} < z < 1$}

For $z \in (\tfrac{1}{2}, 1)$, note that $1 - z \in (0, \tfrac{1}{2})$, so define
\[
  z' = \frac{z}{1 - z} > 1
\]
and run the algorithm on $z'$.
A bounded pair $e/o \approx z'$ satisfies $e/o \approx z/(1-z)$, so
\[
  z \approx \frac{e}{e + o}.
\]
No modification of the parity sequence is required: the sequence for $z'$ is used
solely to produce the counts $e$ and $o$, which then yield the approximant $e/(e+o)$
to the original $z$.

\paragraph{Parity sequence.}
The parity sequence for $z'$ has $\OO$-density
\[
  \frac{1}{1 + z'} = \frac{1}{1 + z/(1-z)} = 1 - z < \tfrac{1}{2},
\]
so it is $\OO\OO$-free and its Sturmian slope is $1 - z$.
The sequence encodes $z'$; the rational approximant $e/(e+o)$ to $z$ is read off
from the $\EE$- and $\OO$-counts of that sequence.

\paragraph{Summary.}
Table~\ref{tab:extension} collects the reductions for all four ranges of $z$.

\section{Conclusion and Open Problems}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%------------------------------------------------------------

We have presented a theory of rational approximation for real numbers based on
bounded pairs and the weighted mediant construction.
The core algorithm applies to $z > 1$; all other real numbers are handled by algebraic
reduction (Section~\ref{sec:extension}).
The central result, Theorem~\ref{thm:optimal-w}, gives a closed-form formula for the
optimal mediant weight at each step, enabling an efficient iterative algorithm.
The parity sequence representation (Section~\ref{sec:parity}) is conjectured to produce
exactly the Sturmian sequence with slope $1/(z+1)$ in the limit
(Conjecture~\ref{prop:sturmian}), with finite prefixes agreeing everywhere except
possibly the terminal symbol; this has been verified computationally but not yet proved.
The mediant tree generates exponentially long prefixes per iteration.
Section~\ref{sec:extension} extends the construction to all $z \in (0,1)$ via the
reductions $z' = (1-z)/z$ (for $z < \tfrac{1}{2}$) and $z' = z/(1-z)$ (for $z > \tfrac{1}{2}$);
in both cases the algorithm runs on the reduced $z' > 1$ and the approximant to $z$
is read off from the resulting $e$ and $o$ counts.

\paragraph{Open problems.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Proof of the Sturmian identity.}
    Conjecture~\ref{prop:sturmian} has two parts, both open.
    First, that finite parity sequences of length $N$ agree with the Sturmian sequence
    at every position except possibly the last: although verified computationally for
    several irrational values ($\sqrt{2}$, $\log_2 3$, $\log_2 5$, $\phi$, $\pi$),
    with the $\log_2 3$ case checked up to $N = 27{,}869{,}189$, no general proof is known.
    Second, that the infinite limit of the mediant construction is exactly the Sturmian
    sequence with slope $1/(z+1)$ for all irrational $z > 1$.
    The mediant construction builds parity sequences via a morphism-based concatenation
    rule; the connection to Sturmian morphisms is the natural proof route.

  \item \emph{The parent-swap phenomenon.}
    To track the Sturmian sequence correctly across successive mediant depths,
    the parent pair $(r_i, r_{i+1})$ must sometimes be swapped when constructing
    child mediants — i.e.\ the roles of the lower and upper bound estimates are
    exchanged at certain levels of the tree.
    Computationally, the condition for a swap is clear: it occurs precisely when
    $\Dval < 0$.
    What has not been established is \emph{why} $\Dval < 0$ is the necessary and
    sufficient condition — i.e.\ a proof that the Sturmian order is preserved if
    and only if the parent pair is swapped at levels where $\Dval < 0$.

\end{enumerate}

\section*{Related Work}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Eliahou~\cite{eliahou1993} studies rational approximation of $\log_2 3$ in the context of
bounding nontrivial cycle lengths in the Collatz problem.
His central rational approximation result (Theorem~3.2) identifies the best upper rational
approximants to an irrational $\theta$ — those fractions $k/l$ with $\theta < k/l \leq \theta'$
minimising $k$ or $l$ — as the \emph{upper convergents} $p_{n,i}/q_{n,i}$ derived from the
continued fraction expansion of $\theta$.
Applied to $\theta = \log_2 3$, this yields explicit lower bounds on Collatz cycle lengths.

Our work differs in two essential respects.
First, our algorithm never computes a continued fraction expansion: it works directly on
bounded pairs via the optimal weight formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:optimal-w}), and the weights
$w_{2n+1}$ emerge from that construction — related to, but not in general identical to, the
continued fraction partial quotients.
Both approaches do, however, share a common dependency: each requires an oracle capable of
supplying an arbitrary-precision representation of the target value $z$, sufficient to
evaluate $\lceil oz \rceil$ (our algorithm) or $\lfloor(n+1)\alpha\rfloor$ (Sturmian)
to the required accuracy.
Neither method generates more precision than the oracle provides.

Second, and more fundamentally, Eliahou's framework provides no means of constructing a
\emph{parity sequence} realising a given approximant.
Knowing that $k/l$ is an upper convergent to $\log_2 3$ says nothing about how to build
an integer whose Collatz trajectory has exactly $l$ odd steps and $k$ even steps arranged
in a valid ($\mathsf{OO}$-free) order.
The mediant tree does exactly that: it simultaneously produces the rational approximant and
the parity sequence that realises it, as a byproduct of the same iterative construction.

More broadly, the mediant tree is a general-purpose encoder: given an oracle for any real
number, it produces a parity sequence over $\{\OO, \EE\}$ that encodes it to arbitrary
precision — the Collatz application is one instance of a universal construction.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:optimal-w}}
\label{app:optimal-w}
%------------------------------------------------------------

We derive the closed-form formula for the real-valued minimiser of the combined
approximation error $\max(|\eps_j|, |\eps_k|)$.

We have a bounded pair $(r_i, r_{i+1}) = (e_i/o_i,\, e_{i+1}/o_{i+1})$ bracketing $z$,
with $|\eps_i| > |\eps_{i+1}|$.
The two weighted mediants are
\[
  r_j = \frac{e_i + (w-1)\,e_{i+1}}{o_i + (w-1)\,o_{i+1}}, \qquad
  r_k = \frac{e_i + w\,e_{i+1}}{o_i + w\,o_{i+1}}.
\]
Write $O_j = o_i + (w-1)\,o_{i+1}$ and $O_k = o_i + w\,o_{i+1}$ for their denominators.

\paragraph{Step 1: Express $\eps_j$ and $\eps_k$ as functions of $w$.}

\[
  \eps_j = \frac{e_i + (w-1)e_{i+1}}{z\,O_j} - 1, \qquad
  \eps_k = \frac{e_i + we_{i+1}}{z\,O_k} - 1.
\]
Define the shorthand
\[
  A = e_i - z\,o_i, \qquad B = e_{i+1} - z\,o_{i+1}.
\]
Note that $A = z\,o_i\,\eps_i$ and $B = z\,o_{i+1}\,\eps_{i+1}$: they are the
signed absolute errors of $r_i$ and $r_{i+1}$, scaled by their respective denominators
and by $z$.
With this shorthand the numerators simplify:
\[
  e_i + (w-1)\,e_{i+1} - z\,O_j
    = (e_i - z\,o_i) + (w-1)(e_{i+1} - z\,o_{i+1}) = A + (w-1)B,
\]
giving
\[
  \eps_j = \frac{A + (w-1)B}{z\,O_j}, \qquad
  \eps_k = \frac{A + wB}{z\,O_k}.
\]

\paragraph{Step 2: Relate $A$, $B$ to $\Delta$ and $K$.}

Recall $\Dval = e_{i+1}o_i - e_io_{i+1}$ and
$\Kval = o_{i+1}(e_{i+1} - zo_{i+1}) = o_{i+1}B$,
so immediately $B = \Kval/o_{i+1}$.
For $A$, compute:
\[
  A\,o_{i+1} - B\,o_i = (e_i - zo_i)o_{i+1} - (e_{i+1} - zo_{i+1})o_i
    = e_i o_{i+1} - e_{i+1}o_i = -\Dval,
\]
hence $A = (Bo_i - \Dval)/o_{i+1}$.
Substituting $B = \Kval/o_{i+1}$:
\begin{align*}
  A + (w-1)B &= \frac{\Kval(o_i/o_{i+1} + w - 1) - \Dval}{o_{i+1}}, \\
  A + wB     &= \frac{\Kval(o_i/o_{i+1} + w) - \Dval}{o_{i+1}}.
\end{align*}
A key identity used in Step~3: for any $O = o_i + c\,o_{i+1}$,
\[
  B\,O - o_{i+1}(A + cB)
    = B\,o_i + B\,c\,o_{i+1} - o_{i+1}\,A - c\,o_{i+1}\,B
    = B\,o_i - o_{i+1}\,A = \Dval.
\]
In particular, $B\,O_j - o_{i+1}(A+(w-1)B) = \Dval$ and
$B\,O_k - o_{i+1}(A+wB) = \Dval$.

\paragraph{Step 3: Set up the minimisation.}

Since $r_j$ and $r_k$ straddle $z$, their errors have opposite signs:
$\eps_j \eps_k \leq 0$.
Minimising $\max(|\eps_j|, |\eps_k|)$ over real $w$ is equivalent to minimising
$|\eps_j \eps_k|$, which (since the product is non-positive) means finding the $w$
at which $\eps_j \eps_k$ is \textbf{maximised} (i.e.\ closest to zero from below).
We set $\frac{d}{dw}(\eps_j \eps_k) = 0$.

\subparagraph{Differentiating $\eps_j$ via the quotient rule.}
Write $\eps_j = N_j/D_j$ where
\[
  N_j = \frac{A+(w-1)B}{z}, \qquad D_j = O_j = o_i + (w-1)\,o_{i+1}.
\]
Both depend on $w$, with derivatives:
\[
  \frac{dN_j}{dw} = \frac{B}{z}, \qquad \frac{dD_j}{dw} = o_{i+1}.
\]
Applying the quotient rule $d(N/D)/dw = (N'D - ND')/D^2$:
\[
  \frac{d\eps_j}{dw}
    = \frac{(B/z)\,O_j - \bigl((A+(w-1)B)/z\bigr)\,o_{i+1}}{O_j^2}
    = \frac{B\,O_j - o_{i+1}(A+(w-1)B)}{z\,O_j^2}.
\]
Applying the identity $B\,O_j - o_{i+1}(A+(w-1)B) = \Dval$ from Step~2:
\[
  \frac{d\eps_j}{dw} = \frac{\Dval}{z\,O_j^2}.
\]
The same calculation for $\eps_k = (A+wB)/(z\,O_k)$ gives:
\[
  \frac{d\eps_k}{dw}
    = \frac{B\,O_k - o_{i+1}(A+wB)}{z\,O_k^2} = \frac{\Dval}{z\,O_k^2}.
\]
Both derivatives share the same numerator $\Dval$ — a direct consequence of
the identity in Step~2, and the reason the product-rule expression simplifies cleanly.

\subparagraph{Applying the product rule.}
By the product rule:
\[
  \frac{d}{dw}(\eps_j\,\eps_k)
    = \frac{\Dval}{z\,O_j^2}\cdot\eps_k + \eps_j\cdot\frac{\Dval}{z\,O_k^2} = 0.
\]
Dividing through by $\Dval/z$ (nonzero since $|\Dval|=1$):
\[
  \frac{\eps_k}{O_j^2} + \frac{\eps_j}{O_k^2} = 0
  \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad
  \eps_k \, O_k^2 = -\eps_j \, O_j^2,
\]
which (since $\eps_j$ and $\eps_k$ have opposite signs) means
$|\eps_k|\,O_k^2 = |\eps_j|\,O_j^2$: the two errors are equal when
weighted by the square of their denominator.

\paragraph{Step 4: Solve the quadratic.}

We need to solve $\eps_k\,O_k^2 + \eps_j\,O_j^2 = 0$.
Substituting from Steps 1 and 2:
\[
  \frac{A + w B}{z\,O_k}\cdot O_k^2 + \frac{A+(w-1)B}{z\,O_j}\cdot O_j^2 = 0
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  (A+wB)\,O_k + (A+(w-1)B)\,O_j = 0.
\]
Expanding using $O_j = o_i + (w-1)o_{i+1}$, $O_k = o_i + wo_{i+1}$,
$A = (Bo_i - \Dval)/o_{i+1}$, and $B = \Kval/o_{i+1}$, then clearing
$o_{i+1}$ denominators, one obtains a quadratic in $w$.
Applying the quadratic formula and simplifying (using $|\Dval| = 1$
from Definition~\ref{def:bounded-pair}) yields the two real roots:
\[
  w = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{o_i}{o_{i+1}}
    + \frac{\Dval \pm \sqrt{\Dval^2 - \Kval^2}}{2\Kval},
\]
completing the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:optimal-w}.

\paragraph{Discriminant and termination.}
The discriminant $\Dval^2 - \Kval^2 = 1 - \Kval^2 \geq 0$
(using $|\Dval|=1$ from Definition~\ref{def:bounded-pair}) since $|\Kval| \leq 1$
throughout the iteration.
\begin{itemize}
  \item When $\Kval = 0$: $r_{i+1}$ is an exact representation of $z$;
    no further improvement is possible and the algorithm terminates.
  \item When $|\Kval| = 1$: the two roots coincide, giving a unique optimal
    weight $w = \tfrac{1}{2} - o_i/o_{i+1} + \Dval/\Kval$.
  \item Otherwise: two real candidates are produced; the correct integer $w$
    is found by taking the floor or ceiling of each and selecting the one that
    keeps the new pair bracketing $z$.
\end{itemize}
